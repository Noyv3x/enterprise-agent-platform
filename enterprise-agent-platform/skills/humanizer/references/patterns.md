# English AI-writing patterns

Condensed and adapted from blader/humanizer 3.1.0 (MIT; see NOTICE.md), whose
taxonomy is based on Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing". Numbered strongest
first: §1–§5 justify an edit on one sighting; *weak alone* means act only when
other tells appear in the same passage. Examples are illustrative; never copy
their facts.

## A. Staging instead of stating

**1. Not X but Y.** not just / not only / not merely X, but Y; it's not X, it's
Y; X rather than Y; the split form ("This does not mean X. It means Y."); a
clipped tail ("…, no guessing"). The negative half names something nobody
claimed so the positive half sounds bigger. State the point. Keep a contrast
only when it corrects a belief the reader really holds or both halves carry
information.
> It's not just a beat; it's part of the atmosphere. → The heavy beat adds to the atmosphere.

**2. One-line closers and dramatic fragments.** "That is the real win."
"That distinction matters." "Let that sink in." A sentence after an example
that names what it showed ("This shows the importance of…"). Rows of fragments
("No prior. No nostalgia."). Cut a closer that repeats; keep it only if it adds
a fact or consequence. Merge fragment rows into one specific sentence.

**3. Sayings that sound deep.** the real question is, at its core, what really
matters, the heart of the matter, X is the language of Y, X becomes a trap.
Replace with the specific claim.

**4. Staged run-up.** Let's dive in, here's what you need to know, without
further ado, Honestly?, Here's the thing, Real talk. Remove the run-up; a
"honestly" inside a casual sentence is fine, the tell is the standalone opener
before a routine claim.

**5. Arguing with no one.** This isn't about…, I'm not saying…, To be clear,
Don't get me wrong, A tempting approach would be…, You might think…, but.
The text rejects an objection or option that appears nowhere else. State the
claim. Keep an objection that is attributed or answered in full.

## B. Rhythm by rule

**6. Forced triads.** Ideas in threes to sound complete ("innovation,
inspiration, and insights"; three parallel examples then a lesson). Check each
item adds a distinct idea; merge, develop the strongest, or vary the shape.
Keep three when there are three.

**7. Repeated sentence openings.** Several sentences in a row with the same
subject. Merge, change subject, or start with the action. Deliberate repetition
for rhythm is fine.

**8. Dashes as universal connector.** Em/en dashes and ` -- ` standing in for a
decision about how clauses relate. Unless the writer's sample uses dashes
(then match its rate), replace with period, comma, colon or parentheses. Leave
dashes inside code, paths, URLs. *Weak alone.*

**9. Stacked qualifiers.** could potentially, might arguably, it's also
possible, to be fair. Keep a qualifier only if the source supports it and the
meaning needs it; keep scope, legal and safety notices. Ordinary hedges
(perhaps, tends to) are human. *Weak alone.*

**10. Hyphenated pairs everywhere.** Hyphen before a noun (a high-quality
report), none after (the report is high quality). Always-hyphenated words
(third-party) keep it. *Weak alone.*

**11. Passive voice and missing subjects.** Name the actor when that is
clearer. *Weak alone.*

## C. Inflation and borrowed authority

**12. Overused words.** additionally, align with, crucial, delve, enduring,
enhance, garner, highlight (verb), interplay, intricate, key (adj.), landscape
(abstract), meticulous, pivotal, showcase, tapestry, testament, underscore
(verb), vibrant, robust/gate (figurative; technical uses are fine). Tells
wherever they appear, especially in groups.

**13. Inflated significance.** stands as a testament, a pivotal moment, plays a
key role, underscores its importance, setting the stage for, evolving
landscape; stock "Challenges and Future Outlook" sections; "the future looks
bright". Keep the fact, drop the significance; end on the last concrete fact.

**14. Vague connection.** associated with, in connection with, linked to. Name
the relationship the source gives; if it does not say, keep the vague wording
rather than inventing a role.

**15. Shallow -ing riders.** …, highlighting / underscoring / ensuring /
reflecting / fostering / showcasing. Keep the fact; keep the rider only when
the source supports it.

**16. Sales language.** nestled, breathtaking, stunning, groundbreaking,
renowned, diverse array, rich cultural heritage. State what the thing is.

**17. Borrowed authority.** experts argue, observers note, industry reports,
a list of prestige outlets, follower counts. If the source names who said what,
use that; otherwise cut. Never manufacture a citation. A missing citation is
not itself a tell.

**18. Avoiding is/are/has.** serves as, stands as, boasts, features, offers.
Use is, are, has.

## D. Formatting by rule

**19. Bold as decoration.** Bold on random words; every list item "**Label:**
sentence". Remove bold; turn labels that carry no information into prose.

**20. Decorative headings.** Title Case On Every Word, emoji or arrows as
decoration, rules between every section, a top heading repeating the title,
headings written for effect. Use sentence case and name what the section holds.

**21. Curly quotes** where the target format uses straight quotes. *Weak alone.*

## E. Leftovers from the chat and the draft

**22. Chatbot residue.** I hope this helps, Certainly!, Great question!, You're
absolutely right, Would you like me to…, let me know. The most certain tell and
easy to miss when wrapped round real content. Remove the wrapper, keep content.

**23. Knowledge-limit disclaimers and guesses.** as of my last update, specific
details are limited, not publicly available, likely grew up…, it is believed
that. State what the source does not show, or cut. Do not fill the gap with a
plausible guess. (A real "this is not documented in the sources we have" is
honest and stays.)

**24. Heading repeated in the first sentence.** `## Performance` followed by
"Speed matters." Drop the restatement.

**25. Writing about the document instead of its subject.** "This function was
added to replace…" (outside change logs); "compiled from…, anything unconfirmed
is flagged rather than guessed"; "the table below compares…". Keep a source
credit the reader can follow and any caveat that changes what they should do;
cut the account of how you worked. One description of the page is *weak alone*.
For this platform: never delete a required disclosure, provenance note or
caveat just because it describes the document.

## F. Writing for the wrong reader

**26. Re-explaining what the reader knows.** A reply that restates the problem,
walks the diagnosis and presents proof before reaching the decision. Act only
when the surrounding conversation is visible or the text is plainly a reply.
Lead with the decision; keep only the reasoning that would change whether the
reader agrees, plus any link they need. If you cannot tell, leave it alone.

## Voice details worth keeping

A specific unusual detail, unresolved mixed feelings, dated slang tied to a
community, a first-person choice the writer can explain, a genuine aside or
self-correction. Do not sand these away.
